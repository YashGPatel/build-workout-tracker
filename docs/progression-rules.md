# Progression rules

Derived from four years of logs (2023–2026) across five programmes. These are
the rules that actually predicted what happened, including the ones that were
wrong first time.

---

## 1. Progress has four axes, not one

Scoring only "did the weight go up" makes real progress look like a stall.
An exercise is progressing if **any** of these improved:

| Axis | Example |
|---|---|
| Load | 25kg → 27.5kg at the same reps |
| Total reps | 12/12 → 14/13 at the same load |
| Rep distribution | 20/18/15 → 18/18/17 — less drop-off across sets |
| Effort at equal work | 15 reps at E10 → 15 reps at E9 |

The fourth one is the easiest to miss and often the first to move.

**An exercise has genuinely stalled only when all four are flat for 2–3
consecutive sessions.** In practice that describes very few exercises at any
given time.

---

## 2. The load-increase trigger

> Add load when you hit the **top of the rep range on every set**, and the
> **first set was rated 9 or easier**.

Effort matters as much as reps. Hitting the top of the range at E10 on every
set means the weight is right and the muscle is at its limit — not that it's
time to add more.

The corollary: if reps go **above** the range, the weight is too light,
regardless of effort.

---

## 3. Increment size dictates rep range

This is the rule that cost the most time to learn, and it broke two exercises
before it was noticed.

> **If the smallest available increment is more than ~20% of your working
> weight, the rep range must be wide enough to absorb it.**

A 15–20 rep range with 5kg loads is a trap. From 5kg hitting 20 reps, the next
rung is 6.25kg — a 25% jump that drops you to ~14 reps, below the range floor.
The progression rule can never fire, so the weight never moves. That is exactly
what happened to lateral raises for four months in 2024: twenty reps, session
after session, with the load drifting *downwards*.

Guidance:

| Increment as % of load | Rep range width |
|---|---|
| Under 10% | 3 reps is fine (10-12) |
| 10–20% | 4–5 reps (10-15) |
| Over 20% | 5+ reps, or change equipment |

**Changing equipment beats widening the range.** A loadable bar or cable with
small plates solves the problem permanently; a wide rep range only tolerates it.

### Where increments come from

- **Dumbbells** — often 1kg steps to 10kg, then 2kg. So 6→8kg is 33%.
- **Cable stacks** — 5kg steps plus *one* hangable plate (1.25 or 2.5kg).
  Achievable: 5, 6.25, 7.5, 10, 11.25, 12.5, 15… **Not** 8.75 or 13.75.
- **Loadable bars** — 2× the smallest plate. A 1.25kg pair gives 2.5kg steps.
- **Fixed EZ bars** — often 5kg apart. Worse than dumbbells; avoid for light work.
- **Microplates** (0.5–1.25kg, magnetic) are the cheapest permanent fix.

---

## 4. Effort management

The effort column maps directly to reps-in-reserve:

| Rating | RIR |
|---|---|
| Failed | past failure |
| 10 (Max Effort) | 0 |
| 9 (Hard) | 1 |
| 8 (Medium) | 2 |
| 7 (Easy) | 3 |

**Only the last set of a muscle group goes to failure.** Taking set 1 to failure
costs reps on sets 2 and 3 — the logs show 20/18/15 patterns collapsing to
12/10/8 when the first set was maxed.

**But this is muscle-dependent.** Forearms and grip tolerate max effort every
set and recover by the next session — they're fatigue-resistant, short range of
motion, and used constantly in daily life. Long-length elbow work (spider curls,
skull crushers, incline curls, overhead extensions) causes far more damage per
set, and grinding those to failure genuinely eats the following session.

---

## 5. Volume is a lever, not a baseline

Add sets when an exercise stalls, not on a schedule. Held in reserve, the third
set is something to spend when progress stops. Spent up front, there's nothing
left when you need it.

There's no strong evidence for scheduled deloads in non-competitive lifters.
Deload for a reason — several lifts falling at once, sleep disrupted, joints
complaining — not on a calendar.

---

## 6. The under-recovery signature

> **Load falling while effort stays at maximum.**

That is the pattern to fear. Not a plateau — a plateau is flat load at steady
effort. Weight going *down* while every set is rated 10 means recovery is the
limiter, and no amount of training adjustment fixes it. In 2024 this ran for
months: lateral raises 7.5 → 5 → 3kg with every set at max effort.

If two or more exercises show it in the same fortnight, the problem is food
and sleep, not programming.

---

## 7. Comparing across time

- **Dumbbell and barbell numbers are portable.** 8kg is 8kg anywhere.
- **Cable numbers are not.** Pulley ratios differ between gyms and often
  between stations in the same gym. A 2:1 pulley makes a 20kg stack 10kg at
  the handle. Never compare stack numbers across gyms, and be cautious across
  machines.
- Ratios cancel when comparing yourself to yourself on the same station, so
  *trends* are always valid even when absolute numbers aren't.
- Record which station or machine an exercise uses. Two exercises with the same
  name on different machines are not the same exercise.

---

## 8. Superset construction

Antagonist pairings do **not** need a load reduction. Each muscle rests while
its opposite works, so per-muscle recovery in a superset with 1–1.5 min between
exercises is roughly 3–3.5 minutes — comparable to straight sets. Wall-clock
time halves; recovery doesn't.

Only same-muscle pairings need the load cut.

Expect systemic and cardiovascular fatigue to be higher for the first 2–3
weeks. If reps fall short early on, that's conditioning, not strength.

**Ordering matters within a session.** Whatever goes second for a given muscle
carries less load and tends to stall. Alternating the order week to week stops
any one exercise living permanently in the fatigued slot.

---

## 9. Reading the notes column

Numbers without context lie. "10 reps at 12kg" reads as a stall until the note
says `LOW ENERGY DAY`, at which point it reads as a solid session under bad
conditions — and the correct response flips from *reduce the weight* to *hold it*.

Worth logging: how you felt, equipment substitutions, grip variations, session
start and end times, and anything that hurt and where.
